Area and background design

Biome 4: El Jardín de Hongos

The mushroom garden, 264 to 376 m. Bounce and breath. Everything here is alive.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

East of the well the mountain is warm and damp, and a forest of mushrooms has grown in the dark: caps as wide as roofs, stalks like tree trunks, vines, roots and moss over every rock. It glows a little, all by itself.

Nothing here was built by people and almost nothing was left by them. The things that live here are snails the size of carts, and the frog, who has a house.

The garden is the gentlest place on the mountain to look at and one of the strangest to climb: the floor throws you, the air lifts you, and the walls are slime.

How it is built

  • Mushrooms are the garden's trees. Each has a stalk, a skirt-ring part way up, a cap, and gills under the cap. Big or small, the same four parts.
  • The mushroom you bounce on: an orange cap with pale spots, a cream stalk and cream gills, in every size. No checked or striped caps (note 37).
  • A second kind, for scenery only (Parker, 11-13): tall and thin, with a small pale blue-green cap like a bell, growing in stands. It makes the far forest. It never grows in the play space, and nothing orange grows in the scenery, so an orange cap always means bounce.
  • Shelf fungus grows out of walls in steps, like brackets. Roots and vines hang like ropes. Mycelium, white and stringy, ties things together like netting.
  • Rock is rounded and mossy, never cut. Slime is black and runs down in sheets.
  • The only made thing is the frog's hut: a door and a round window cut into a fat stalk, a cap for its roof, a lantern made of a spore pod.

Colour and light

A green glow from moss and from spore lanterns, with the orange of the caps as the one warm colour. The glow comes from below and from the sides, so caps are lit on their undersides. Spores drift everywhere, slowly. The dark stretch (Los Ojos) is dim, not black, and the light fades in and out of it over several metres (note 45).

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: Out of the air: you land from the first well crossing in a curtain of roots on the east rim. The crystal is behind you across the shaft; moss, roots and the first three caps are in front.

Leaving: By the second crossing of the well, thrown west off the great cap. The far rim is cut stone: the temple's door, with two banner chains hanging into the shaft to catch. Roots and small mushrooms have got into the stonework on that side, so the garden reaches a little way into the temple.

Running through it

  • The well: the second of the three crossings, and the widest view down it.
  • The frog: here it has a house you can walk into.
  • The ones who tried: one frozen climber, half grown over with moss.
  • The snails: one far back in most areas, asleep, before you meet the ones you climb.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
