Area and background design

Biome 11: La Cumbre

The summit, 962 to 1,000 m, above the clouds. The game's ending. One dome of black ice, a crown of gold, the ropes of the first ring, a last leap to a bell rope, and the belt on a post.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

Above the storm there is no weather. The cloud lies below you like a white floor, the sun is low and gold, and the last 38 m of the mountain stand up out of it: one dome of black ice with snow on its back, and, standing clear of the dome on its east side, a ring of five rock teeth and two horns of rock. The dome pours east, off a prow and down past the storm ridge. So every miss up here is the same fall: the dome, the prow, the whole mountain.

This is the First Top Dog's ring. He was the first to climb the Peak, and he would fight nowhere else, so anyone who wanted his belt had to make the climb first. The people who built the temple to him forged the belt in the chimney and carried a ring up here in pieces. They capped the five teeth with gold for a crown, stood the corner posts on the horns, strung the ropes, laid the canvas, and hung a bell on the very top to start and end a bout. Nobody knows that a challenger ever got this far. The belt has waited on the bell's post ever since.

The weather has had the ring for a long time. What is left of it: two corner posts standing on the horns and a third fallen on the shelf below them; six ropes, still tight on the horns' inner faces and still lively enough to throw a luchador; their turnbuckle pads; one strip of canvas; the bell; the belt. The last of the ones who tried sits 4 m under the bell, looking up at it. One frog lives up here. It keeps a tea shop on the ledge under the prow, and it is the one who hangs the town's paper fresh and lays the marigolds. Under the west shoulder, where a body lands only by falling the wrong way off the very top, there is a warm spring that the frogs have kept to themselves.

How it is built

  • Black ice over rock, the glacier's own: blue-black and glossy, in flat planes with a hard white glint. The dome, its prow, the tunnel under it, the west shoulder and both horns are all of it, and none of it can be held. Snow lies only on the dome's back and on the shoulder, as a white cap set about 1 m back from the line a body slides on, so that line is always a dark wet rim.
  • Summit rock is warm, pale and dry, the colour of old cream. It shows only where you stand: the tea ledge, the shelf under the horns, the west horn's top, and the perch under the bell. Roots stand one layer back. The body of every tooth and the root of each horn is a flat rock card 1.4 to 1.8 m behind the climb, and only the tip is solid. A sliding body passes in front of them, so nothing on the east side can stop a fall.
  • The temple's people left gold and bronze. An eight-sided gold boss is pinned to the tip of each tooth, brushed and matte. The bell is bronze, with the First Top Dog's face cast on it. The belt is gold plates on red leather.
  • The ring is honey timber, hemp, iron and padded cloth. A corner post is square timber 0.4 m thick with three iron turnbuckle eyes and a red pad on its top. A ring rope is nothing like a rope you climb: it is a band 0.3 m thick, wound in glossy tape, with a fat cushion over the turnbuckle at each end. Each horn carries three, one above the other like the three ropes of a ring's side: red at the bottom, cream in the middle, jade at the top. The canvas is cream, with a faded pug's face.
  • Rime is the summit's own stuff: hard grey-white frost that the wind builds in feathers on anything that stands in it. It makes a collar under each gold boss, a nose on the tea ledge's end and a block on the shelf. It is pale and matte, cracked across with a corner gone, and it grows toward the west, into the wind.
  • The bell frame is the biggest made thing: a post 6.6 m tall footed in the very top, a beam 6.8 m long and one brace, in honey timber, with marigolds wound up the post.
  • Paper and marigolds are the town's, whole and bright. Every line of paper is tied on thick old ring rope, 2 m behind the climb, and passes at least 2.5 m above any place you can stand, so no line reads as a way across. The line to the bell is carried on a flagstaff lashed behind the corner post. Garlands swag from tooth to tooth, 2 m back.
  • The tea shop is the frog's own work: planks, a striped cloth, a tin stove pipe, set in a recess in the tea ledge's back wall and cut away at the front. The spring's cave is the mountain's inside, warm: rounded ochre and cream rock crusted with mineral, with no ice in it.
  • Never here: steel, brick, painted metal, storm cloud, lightning, a cloud or a line of paper in front of the climb, a glowing material, or a rope that looks like a ring rope and can be held.

Colour and light

Gold, from one low sun in the east: the far view's own sun. There is no second sun and no sky card of ours. Everything that faces east is lit warm: the teeth, the horns' roots, the bell. West sides and undersides are in long blue-violet shadow, so the prow's underside, the tunnel and the west shoulder are the dark parts of the picture. Gold dust drifts in the air. Two places have a warm light of their own, shown as amber colour on their walls: the tea shop, from its lantern and its stove, and the frogs' spring, from three candle lanterns and from daylight coming down the chute.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: From the storm ridge's headwall, up the last broken rungs of its iron ladder, onto the east end of a rock ledge at 964 m. This side of the join is ours. The ledge is 17 m long, and the prow of the summit's dome roofs it, 4 to 5 m overhead. You come up out of grey cloud into gold light, with the cloud sea at your feet to the east. The first things you see are a line of whole bright paper across the ledge's mouth, a thread of steam, and the tea shop's lantern in the back wall. Two things keep the ledge from being quite safe: the black mouth of the tunnel at its west end, where whatever falls from higher up comes sliding out across the floor, and the dome's whole fall line pouring off the lip over your head. The storm ridge's side of the join is for its own sheet.

Leaving: There is no biome above. You leave by the ending: challenge 11.6, Top Dog, and note 101. From the bell you climb onto the beam and along it to the post's top, and you touch the belt. Then a ceremony plays on you. The belt comes off the post into your hands and you lift it over your head with both arms. You turn slowly on the spot and rise off the post into the air above the summit. Long flat rays fan out from you and wheel as you turn. The orchestra plays the mountain's call, resolved, and the bell tolls three times. The town's fireworks come up through the cloud far below. Then the TOP DOG card with your time and your falls, the confetti, and the wait for a button, as today. From then on the Peak behind the title screen flies your flag. How long the ceremony runs, and whether a button skips it, are settled when it is built.

Running through it

  • The ring: what the whole climb was for. The temple tested you, the forge made the belt, and this is the ring the belt was fought for in. The belt was made in the chimney's forge, 500 m below, and it waits on the bell's post: the last thing in the game.
  • The pug: cast on the bell, on the belt's centre plate, faded on the canvas.
  • The ones who tried: one, the last. He sits on a perch 4 m under the bell, looking up, with marigolds laid beside him. There is nobody above him.
  • The town follows you up: its paper is whole and bright here for the first time since the temple, and its marigolds are on the crown and the bell. When you take the belt its fireworks come up through the cloud. The town itself is under the cloud and not seen from here: the fireworks are how you know it saw you.
  • The fall: the dome under every move, pouring east. The last leap is the worst fall in the game, with the bell in view. The mercy is one iron ring under the prow's lip.
  • Tea: the mountain's second tea place, and one thread with the tea cave on the wind cliffs. It is the same pot, the same cups and the same kettle. There it was a low table and two cups in a cave; here it is a counter, a row of cups and a sign. A cup is set out for you both times.
  • The frog: this biome's frog keeps the tea shop, in an apron. And the secret of all the frogs is here: the hot spring under the west shoulder, full of them.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
