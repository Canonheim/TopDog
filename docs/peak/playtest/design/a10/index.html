Area and background design

Biome 10: La Cresta de la Tormenta

The storm ridge, 884 to 966 m. Wind, lightning, and long flings in both. One face of wet slate with rock towers standing in the air east of it. Every miss here is the whole mountain.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

Above the glacier the mountain narrows to a ridge, and the storm sits on it and never leaves. The ridge is one face of wet slate, 78 m tall, leaning back to the west. Nothing on it grips. Whatever touches it slides to its foot, where a prow of rock, El Alero, throws it out over the glacier's long slide. East of the face, rock towers stand in the air with their feet in cloud. They are the only way up.

The town came this far once. Its cableway runs on wind, so it wanted to know the weather, and it built a weather station on one of the towers: a hut, a mast to take the lightning, a wind vane as big as a house, kites and a balloon to measure the air higher up. A keeper lived in a cabin under El Alero, out of the rain, and went to work along ropes strung from tower to tower. The town tied its cut paper to his ropes so that he could find them in cloud. Now every paper is a prayer for somebody who went up.

Then the ice came down over the cableway and nobody came to relieve him. The storm has run the station ever since. Lightning burnt the hut and still takes the mast every six seconds. The vane has never stopped, and the kites and the balloon are still flying on their lines. Some of the prayer lines have snapped. The cabin still has a fire in its stove, and only a frog lives in it. Above the last tower the cloud ends, and gold light comes down the headwall from the summit.

How it is built

  • The rock is slate. It splits into long flat slabs that lean the way the face leans, so everything natural here is a slab, a blade or a square-topped tower. Wet slate is blue-black and glossy. Dry slate is pale warm grey and matte, broken in square steps. It is found only on the flat tops of towers and on the pale scars where a flake has come away. Knots of white quartz stand 0.3 m proud of the slate, and they are holds.
  • A tower is the near edge of a bigger body of rock. The part you climb is undercut to a slick point, so that a falling body passes under it and behind it. The tower's body carries on 3 m behind, duller and greyer, and goes down into painted cloud. It never ends in the air. Only the last tower, the buttress, leans on the face.
  • Walls and masses are painted, not built of blocks (Parker, 11-13). The face, El Alero and every tower are wet slate in a few big leaning planes. The slate's cleavage is flat colour on those planes, in three or four blue-black tones: long wavy lines and bands that run the way the face leans. Nothing painted narrows to a point, and no rain is painted on the rock. The rain is the moving effect in the air in front of it.
  • Only a few big breaks are real shape: one leaning slab standing off a tower, one opened joint with a painted shadow in it, the undercut over a tower's pointed foot, a pale scar where a flake has gone. None of them is on the face, where nothing may catch a falling body. Away from the tops, the scars and the knobs, a tower's outline leans and is notched, and is never a ruled upright. Never a repeated square panel, and never a slab built as its own block.
  • The station is the town's work, by the same hands as the cableway: honey timber in square posts and thick planks, pegged, and bolted through iron plates with the pug's face on them, with red paint and a cream band on what moves. It is storm-beaten. Plank ends are split, the hut has lost half its roof to fire, and every burnt edge is black.
  • Steel comes in two kinds that never look alike. Bare steel is pale blue-grey, cold and matte, thin and round, with rivet heads. It is only on the mast, and the lightning takes it. Greased steel is black and glossy and cannot be held: the vane's arms, and the line pole on El Filo. Wherever bare steel meets wood there is a stack of white ribbed porcelain.
  • Rope is hemp. A prayer line is a hemp rope with cut paper hung along it, in the town's colours at their loudest. A whole prayer line is always a hold. A line that is only scenery is never whole: it is a torn end streaming from a post, or it is painted on the far card.
  • Cloud is painted as cloud: flat cut-out banks with round heads and a scalloped underside, dark below and paler on top, in mid greys and slate blues, with nothing near white. Cloud stands behind the climb, across the bodies of the towers. Nothing made of cloud ever stands in front of where a climber can be (notes 40 and 92).
  • What is drawn is what can be touched. The vane's cups and arms, the kites, the balloon and its basket are each modelled at exactly the size the course gives them, and no other part of them is solid.
  • Never here: brick, carved stone, ice you can hold, anything green, a lit lamp outside the cabin, a cloud card in front of the climb, or a thin line hanging behind the climb that is not a rope.

Colour and light

Storm grey, with white flashes. There is no sun and almost no shadow: flat grey-blue light from above, darker toward the foot of the ridge, with rain falling through it up to 952 m. The far cloud flickers pale now and then, small and silent. The whole picture flashes only when the mast is struck: for an instant everything turns pale and throws a hard black shadow. That is a change of colour, never a glow. The only warm light on the ridge is the cabin's: amber from its stove and one lamp, shown as colour on its walls. Above 952 m you come out of the top of the cloud. The rain stops, and gold light from the summit comes down the headwall.

It blowsTen winds lie one above the other across the whole ridge, in bands 5 to 15 m tall. Each blows one way only, and they take turns up the ridge: east, west, east. Each gusts for about 2 seconds and rests for about 3.5. A gust is a stripe of long wavy white lines with an inked edge, exactly as tall as its band and as wide as the screen, moving the way it blows (note 89). The rain says it too: in a band that is blowing the rain leans hard that way, and in a still band it falls straight, so the bands show as stripes of rain. Each band has its own flags where the route crosses it: the papers on a prayer line, a torn end on a post, a streamer pole at the back of a rest, the cabin's smoke, a kite's tail. They hang slack between gusts. In the 0.8 seconds before a gust the band's flags lift, a whistle comes, and a front of lines crosses from the side the wind is coming from. Where the lines stop, the wind stops. At 943 to 953 m two winds blow at each other and meet between the two lone towers: their lines curl upward where they meet, and the torn ends on those two towers' posts point at each other.
It strikesLightning takes one thing on the ridge: the weather mast of El Rayo, every 6 seconds. The lightning's metal is bare steel: pale blue-grey, cold, thin and round, with rivet heads, and scorched black at its joints. That is the six rungs, the bare lengths of the spine, and the rod at the tip. Wood is safe: honey timber, thick and square, with grain and pegs. That is the deck, the three crossarms, and the sleeve round the spine above each. White porcelain marks every place where the two meet. For 1.5 seconds before a strike you are told three ways. Blue-white sparks start at the deck and crawl up the steel to the tip, faster and thicker, and a brush of sparks stands on the end of every rung. A hum rises. And the button mark on any limb of yours that is on steel buzzes (note 95). Then a drawn bolt, forked, white with an inked edge, comes down from the dark cloud to the tip, and the steel is white and covered in sparks for the 0.4 seconds it is live. All of this is colour and sparks, never glow. A burnt black star on the deck round the mast's foot and a pug sign with a bolt on the hut's wall say it before you start. No spark ever crawls on anything but the mast.
It movesThe storm drives everything that moves, and you can see how. The vane: three black arms with a red and cream cup on each, turning once in 5 seconds on a banded mast that stands on the station tower's body. Only the cups are held. A kite: a paper diamond flying a figure of eight you can read, with a 6 m paper tail. Its string runs straight back to a reel on the tower's body, so nothing hangs under a kite but its tail. The balloon: it bobs 2 m on a thick black tether from a winch on the buttress, with one hemp rope hung from the middle of its basket (note 96).
It will breakThe mountain's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone. Here it is two of the headwall's four iron rungs: eaten thin, cracked through, hanging by one bolt with the other end dropped, and shedding flakes of rust. Each holds for 1.2 seconds. A sound rung is straight, thick, and bolted at both ends.
It is only cloudCloud is always behind you. It has round heads and soft scallops, no straight edge, and no top you could stand on. It is greyer than dry slate and never glossy. The towers' bodies sink into it, and rain and wind lines pass in front of it. In La Nube (10.7) you are inside the cloud: there one bank fills the whole frame behind you, 1.5 m back, the far view is gone, and the five quartz knobs stand on a rib of dark rock that shows plainly against it. Nothing hides you or the holds. No place on the ridge is climbed blind today. If one is ever wanted, the scene dims and the mast's lightning shows the holds, and no card stands in front (note 92).
It is sceneryAnything a step or more behind the climb. It is duller, greyer and cooler than what you climb: the towers' bodies, the ridge's flank, the hut, the vane's mast, the kite reels, the winch and its tether, the cabin's walls. Behind those: painted cloud, the far towers, and the storm.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: From the glacier's top by one fling (challenge 9.10): off the raised mitten of the last frozen climber, 7 m west and 3.4 m up across the gap, onto the ledge at 886 m under El Alero. You land past its glazed first metre. What you fly at is the cabin: its stove and lamp are the only warm colour in a grey picture, and its smoke, streaming east, shows you the first wind before you land. The ice ends at the ledge's lip and wet slate begins. The ledge is the cabin's porch and it is not safe: the lowest wind blows east only, off the ledge. The way on is the torn prayer line that hangs under the prow's tip, a hand past the ledge's east end.

Leaving: By challenge 10.9, Escalera al Cielo: from the buttress's top step at 955.5 m, four iron rungs up the last of the face in a wind that peels you off it, then out round the east end of the exit ledge and onto it at 964 m. The ledge is 8 m long, overhangs the face by 2 m, and is roofed by the prow of the summit's snow dome. Our end of the join: the slate and the rain end below you, the top of the storm is under your feet, the light is gold from above, and the paper line over the ledge is whole, bright and dry. Note 97 makes that ledge a little tea shop with a frog, and that is for the summit's sheet. A fall from the rungs is still the whole mountain.

Running through it

  • The fall line: under every move is the face, then El Alero's brink, then the glacier's slide. From the towers you can see all of it.
  • The town follows you up: here its cut paper is the prayer lines. It is torn at the cabin, whole between the towers, flown as kites, and whole and dry at the exit ledge.
  • The cableway's wind: the vane is the big sister of the cableway's wind engines, in the same red and cream.
  • The pug: on the vane's hub, on the bolt sign and the arrow sign, cut into the kites, and on the iron plates at the station's joints.
  • The ones who tried: one frozen climber sits on the station's deck against the hut, facing the mast, with a hand on the timber. He was waiting for the lightning to stop.
  • The town below: gone. From the cabin's ledge it is a last strip at the foot of the picture, and then there is only cloud until the summit.
  • Tea: the keeper's kettle is the same one as in the drivers' cave on the cliffs and in the glacier's snow hole.
  • The frog: in the keeper's cabin on the first ledge, at the table under the lamp, in a rain hat. You see its light from the glacier's top.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
  13. Walls and rock are painted, not built of blocks (Parker, 11-13: "I like the painted look"). A wall, a cliff or any mass you climb is a few big planes with a designed, uneven outline. Its layers and courses are flat painted colour on those planes, as long wavy lines and bands, with no ink inside a face and nothing that tapers to a point (no streaks or wedges: "it does not need these random triangles ... just the painted lines look lovely"). Only a few big breaks are real shape: an overhang, a deep recess, a ledge, a leaning slab. No repeated square panel, no long ruled edge. The holds and the tops you stand on stay exactly where the course has them. The mass's own body carries on behind it, duller, as one big form. Worked example: the split tower top of the wind cliffs (design/walls_example_before.jpg and walls_example_after.png).
