La Cresta de la Tormenta · Area and background design

Challenge 10.3: El Rayo

Design only. Nothing here is built, and nothing is modelled until the whole mountain has been playtested.

The place, in one paragraph

The town's weather station, on a tower of its own. The hut stands on the tower's body and its floor runs forward as a plank deck. In front of the hut stands the mast that was built to take the lightning, so that nothing else would be struck: a spine of bare steel 16 m tall with a rod on its tip, built like a telegraph pole, with three timber crossarms that carried the station's instruments. The keeper had to climb it, so he boxed the steel in timber wherever he had to stand, and put white porcelain between the timber and the steel. It worked, and it still works. Lightning burnt half the hut's roof off years ago and takes the mast every 6 seconds. One of the ones who tried sits on the deck with his back to the hut and his hand on the first timber sleeve, looking up at the tip. He was waiting for it to stop. The way up is from timber to timber, two steel rungs at a time, between strikes.

What must not change

  • Every piece of the mast where it is: the deck, four timber sleeves, three timber crossarms, the bare steel spine, six steel rungs, and the tip.
  • The lightning: every 6 seconds, with 1.5 seconds of warning, live for 0.4 seconds. It takes steel only, and timber is safe.
  • The frozen climber, seated where he is.
  • The wind from 914 to 926 m, which blows east.

The three layers

Background

  • 1The ridge's far towers. The kit's one painted card, 14 m back: the ridge's other towers going away along the crest, with their feet in painted cloud and the back ones hazed to the storm's grey. Behind them the storm's own deck is at its darkest over the mast. The mast's steel is seen against that dark. (14.0 to 14.2 m behind you)
  • 2The ridge's flank. Leaning ribs of slate behind the open air just east of the face, 3 m back. It runs off the west of the frame. (3.0 to 3.6 m behind you)
  • 3The station tower's body, and the cloud at its foot. The body the tower is the near edge of, 12 m wide at the top, with the saw-toothed shoulder the vane's mast stands on. Its lower end sinks into a collar of cloud. The tower's slick point hangs in front of the collar. (1.9 to 3.8 m behind you)
  • 4The hut. The kit's ruined hut, 3.6 m wide and 2.6 m tall, on the tower's body. The deck is its floor carried forward. The east half of its roof, the half beside the mast, is burnt away: bare black rafter ends, and every burnt edge is black. The west half still has its slates. It is weathered grey, a step duller than the deck. (1.4 to 3.4 m behind you)
  • 5The bolt sign. Note 95. The mine's skull-pug board with one change: a white lightning bolt painted across it. It hangs on the hut's wall between the mast and the corner where you arrive, at head height, facing you. You read it before you touch the mast. (1.3 to 1.4 m behind you)
  • 6The rain gauge and the cold lantern. The station's small things, against the hut's east corner: the brass rain gauge on its stand, and a storm lantern on the eave, dead and dented. The gauge stands in the play space today. It moves back 0.5 m, out of it. (1.3 to 1.7 m behind you)
  • 7What the crossarms carried. Each crossarm is the front end of a timber yard that runs 2.4 m back, and an instrument still hangs under each yard's far end. Behind the first: a small louvred box for the thermometer, grey slats. Behind the second: a storm cone of black canvas, point down, the signal the station flew to warn the cableway of wind. It has been up for years. Behind the third, the nest: a plank stage 1.2 m deep with a burnt hole in it, the keeper's top landing. None of them is steel, and no spark ever crawls on them. (1.4 to 2.6 m behind you)
  • 8The vane's mast, and two socks. Note 89. The vane's banded mast stands on the body's shoulder, 7 m east of the lightning mast and 2.5 m back. The mast's wind has its flags here, clear of the climb: two grey canvas socks on the vane's mast, at 916 and 919 m, below the vane's sweep. They stream east. Nothing that flaps is put on the lightning mast itself. (2.3 to 2.8 m behind you)

Play space

  • 9Timber. Note 95. Everything safe is honey timber: the deck, the four sleeves and the three crossarms. It is thick and square, 0.6 m across on the sleeves, with grain, pegs and split ends, and it is warm in a cold picture. A sleeve is a box of four planks round the spine, so you can see that the steel is inside it and that your hands are not on it.
  • 10Steel. Note 95. Everything the lightning takes is bare steel: the six rungs, the three bare lengths of the spine, and the rod on the tip. It is pale blue-grey, cold and matte, thin and round, half as thick as the timber, with rivet heads, and scorched black at every joint. It holds, and it kills on the strike. Timber and steel differ in colour, in thickness and in shape, so the mast still reads with the colour taken away.
  • 11Porcelain. Note 95. A stack of white ribbed porcelain at each of the seven places where steel meets timber: the top of each sleeve and the underside of each crossarm. Each stack is drawn on the timber's side of the joint. So a hand on white is a hand on timber, and the steel begins exactly where the white ends. There are three stacks today.
  • 12The burnt star. A black star 2 m across, burnt into the deck's planks round the mast's foot. It says what the mast does before the first strike.
  • 13The warning and the strike, drawn. Note 95. For the 1.5 seconds before a strike, blue-white sparks start at the deck and crawl up the steel to the tip, faster and thicker, and a brush of sparks stands on both ends of every rung. A hum rises with them. The button mark on any limb of yours that is on steel buzzes. Then the bolt: forked, white with an inked edge. It starts in the dark cloud far behind the ridge and comes forward as it comes down, so it passes behind the two lone towers above and behind anyone in the air between them, and reaches the play space only in its last 3 m, at the tip. The steel is white and covered in sparks for the 0.4 seconds it is live. All of it is colour and sparks, never glow.
  • 14The one who waited. The frozen climber sits where the course has him, on the deck with his back to the hut. One hand rests on the first timber sleeve and his mask is turned up to the tip. His hand is on timber. That is the lesson, and he is right about it.
  • 15The winds, drawn. Note 89. The mast's wind, from 914 to 926 m, as long wavy white lines moving east. The rope way's wind shows below it going west, and the vane's wind above it going west. The wind lines are white and long, and the sparks are blue-white, short and only on steel, so the two are never taken for each other.

Small effects, no models

  • The whole picture goes pale for an instant at each strike and everything throws a hard black shadow. It is a change of colour, never a glow.
  • A wisp of smoke from the tip and from the burnt star after each strike.
  • Thunder with the strike, at once. This is the one place on the ridge where they come together.
  • Rain leaning east across the mast's band.
  • The frozen climber's mask is lit white by each strike. He does not move.

Tonight's notes folded in

  • Note 95, all five parts: steel looks like steel and timber like timber; sparks crawl up the steel for the 1.5 seconds of warning; the bolt is drawn and the steel stays lit while it is live; the button mark on a limb on steel buzzes; a pug sign with a bolt hangs on the hut, and the frozen climber shows where a hand is safe.
  • The note's plan put the frozen climber's hand on the first rung. The first rung is 2 m above the deck and is steel. As the sheet has it, his hand rests on the timber sleeve.
  • The sheet lists the frozen climber as scenery behind the deck. In the course he is a real thing in the play space that can be held, and this design leaves him there.
  • The bolt: the course draws it from a point above and west of the mast. A straight line from there to the tip passes 0.1 m from the top of the second lone tower, where a climber stands. Here the bolt comes from far behind and reaches the play space only at the tip, so what is drawn touches only what it strikes.
  • Note 89: the mast's wind is drawn across its whole band, and its flags stand on the vane's mast, not on the lightning mast.
  • Course changes, scenery only: the rain gauge moves back 0.5 m, out of the play space. Seven porcelain stacks in place of three, each on the timber's side of its joint.
  • Nothing you touch moves.
