Area and background design

Biome 6: La Chimenea

The chimney, 476 to 588 m. Heat, then the sky. A forge at the bottom of a natural flue that ends in daylight.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

East of the well's mouth the mountain has a throat of its own: a volcanic flue, hot at the bottom, open to the sky 100 m up. The temple's smiths built their forge in the foot of it, because the mountain did the heating for them. This is where the First Top Dog's belt was made.

The forge is black iron and soot-black stone: steam grates in the floor, a bellows as big as a house, slag pans, three drop hammers on chains, anvils. Above the forge the made things thin out and the flue is bare rock with a few iron rings, and above that there is only a crack with daylight in it.

So this biome is a journey in itself: from the last room that the temple's people built, up through their iron, out of everything anybody made, into the open air.

How it is built

  • At the bottom, the temple's own stonework, soot-black: the same blocks and stepped arches as across the well, with the carving worn nearly away.
  • Then iron. Black riveted plate, thick chain, grates of square bar, round rings on wall pins, pipes with rib bands. All heavy and plain: this iron was made here, by hand. Sound iron you can hold is rust orange where hands and weather have worn it; the rest is black.
  • The big machines stand on stone footings with a pug's face in iron on each: the bellows, the hammers, the slag pans.
  • Above the forge, basalt: grey-brown rock in rough blocky steps, split by heat. The higher you go the paler and drier it gets.
  • No brick, no timber except tool handles and the bellows' boards, no crystal, nothing green.

Colour and light

The light is the story of the biome. At the bottom it is furnace red from below, with black shadows above. It climbs through orange to a dusty gold. At the top a slit of blue-white daylight comes down the flue, and by the last challenge the rock is lit from above and the red is gone.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: From the temple, across the well's mouth on the censers. You land on soot-black temple stone beside the first steam grate. The torches stop and the red light from below takes over.

Leaving: Through the crack to daylight, onto the cliffs. The rock goes from basalt to pale chalk in the crack's last few metres, the air turns blue, wind comes in, and the whole valley opens with the town tiny below. The cliffs are the next biome and are not in this run; the crack itself is designed here as challenge 6.9.

Running through it

  • The slit of sky, which you can see from the forge floor and which grows the whole way up.
  • The pug: in iron, on every machine's footing.
  • The ones who tried: a frozen climber sitting at the anvil corner, and one more higher up.
  • The town follows you up: one scorched rag of bunting.
  • The frog: in the smith's hut. Only the frog lives there.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
