Area and background design

Biome 0: La Estación

The trailhead at the edge of town, and the cart ride into the mountain. The only biome in the open air until the cliffs.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

This is where the mine's ore used to come out and where the town ends. A loading platform, a siding with one cart on it, a tunnel mouth, and the Peak standing over all of it. The mine shut long ago. The station is kept up now by nobody in particular, for the luchadores who come to climb: somebody still hangs bunting, somebody still oils the cart's brake.

It is the last place that looks like the town: plaster, tiles, paint and noon sun. From the tunnel mouth inward, everything is the mine's.

The ride is the way in: 115 m of track, a dip, a banked drop, a broken rail the cart really hops (with dust off its wheels as it lands), one look at the waterfall cavern through a gap, and the buffer that tips you out.

How it is built

  • Town building: thick plastered walls in cream, terracotta roof tiles, painted woodwork, all in the plaza's own painted-cardboard look.
  • Station gear in honey timber and red paint: the loading platform on posts, ore crates, barrels, a hand crane, the cart, its red brake lever.
  • The tunnel mouth is the first timber set of the mine: the same posts and cap as every set inside, here whitewashed, with a painted pug over the cap.
  • Down the tunnel the same sets go from whitewashed, to bare, to grey and wet. That is the whole of the change from town to mine, and it happens while you ride.

Colour and light

Noon sun, the brightest place in the game: cream walls, a blue sky, short hard shadows. In the tunnel the daylight shrinks to a dot behind you and the lanterns take over, one every twenty metres, each a little dimmer and more amber than the last.

How it tells you what a thing is

What it isHow it looks here
You can hold itHoney timber, crates, barrels, pale dry rock, the cart's wooden sides, rope.
You can'tAlmost nothing here: one streak of black mud in the tunnel, so the first slick thing you ever see is seen from a moving cart.
It movesThe cart. You start it by pulling the red brake lever, whether you are in the cart or not: your hand takes the lever the way it takes a chair, for as long as you hold that limb's button. Red means: this does something.
It is sceneryThe town and the mountain: far, flat and sun-bleached. Near scenery (the station house, the fence) is plaster and tile, which nothing you climb is made of.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: You start here, standing in the sun at the edge of town, facing the mountain.

Leaving: The cart's tunnel is the join with the mine. The timber sets go from whitewashed to rotten as you ride, the lanterns go from bright to amber, the bunting stops, and the gap shows you the waterfall cavern before you are in it. It is designed as its own page: the join from the station to the mine.

Running through it

  • The Peak itself, seen whole for the only time until the ending.
  • The town follows you up: the bunting starts here, torn.
  • The pug: painted over the tunnel mouth, and on the arrow sign.
  • The ones who tried: a few Missing posters, each a masked luchador in pixel art, on the station wall.
  • The frog: on the station house windowsill, in plain sight. It teaches you to look for the others.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
