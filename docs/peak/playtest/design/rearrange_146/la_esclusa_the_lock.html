Challenge 1.13: La Esclusa, the lock

Design only. Nothing here is built. Numbers marked first guess are to be tuned by playing it.

The place, in one paragraph

The lock that let the barrels down from the river to the flume. It is a stone chamber 22 m long and 13 m deep under the end of the black waters' river, where the river used to pour into the mine. The black waters' slot still brings it down, now onto a flap gate at the chamber's top. The old frog keeper still works it from his booth on the quay: he hauls his lever, the flap drops, the river comes through as a white column, and the chamber fills 11 m in six seconds. Then it holds, the sluices in the lower gate open, and it drains away. You go in through the wicket at the bottom, cross while it is low, ride the water up, and at the very top of a fill reach out of the old dry shaft onto the lip ledge where El Borde starts.

What must not change about the play

  • A fall from El Borde lands in this lock (it used to go on down the slot to the Sump): gentler, and kept (Parker, 11-14 15:30).
  • El Borde exactly as today: its lip ledge (x -232 to -226, y 90), the slot, the pier with its peaked top, the dry shaft, the river, its one chain.
  • The way into El Borde is still up the old dry shaft onto the lip ledge, as from the mine today.

The cycle

The pitch's cycle is 14 s: 6 s rising, 3 s held, 5 s draining. That leaves no time at the bottom, so the wicket would never be open and nobody could cross before the fill. I added 4 s at the bottom (first guess), so the cycle is 18 s. The keeper hauls his lever 1 s before each fill. The lip ledge is in reach for 3.6 s of each cycle: the last moment of the rise and the 3 s hold.

The four layers

1. The painted backdrop

far, 12 m and more behind: one painted scene, never a flat colour

  • 1The river's culverts, through a grating. High in the chamber's back wall, a barred opening onto the culverts the river runs through behind the lock: painted going back in rows, lit teal from far off, with the sound of the water. The one look out of the chamber. (12.0 to 16.0 m behind you)

2. The painted set

the near back wall and its depth, 2 to 10 m behind

  • 2The chamber's back wall. Cut stone in long courses, stained teal up to the highest water and green-black below the lowest, with a pale scum line painted where the water stands at the top of each fill. Rust tracks run down from the ledges' brackets. The two ends of the chamber are the lower gate (west) and plain wet rock (east). (1.9 to 2.2 m behind you)
  • 3The quay's back wall and the keeper's booth. Brick, with the keeper's booth set into it beside the lower gate: a little stone room with a round-headed opening, a lamp, a stool and a window onto the chamber. Cut away at the front, so you see in. (1.3 to 2.6 m behind you)
  • 4The slot's foot and the flap gate. Where the black waters' slot comes down through the roof: an iron frame round its foot and a heavy flap gate on a hinge, shut across it. When it drops, the river comes through as a white column into the chamber. When it is shut, the river slides off it into the bypass culvert's mouth at the back of the slot, which takes it down to the flume (and the barrels with it). (1.2 to 2.0 m behind you)

3. Real things

the hero piece and the things round the climb

  • 5The frog keeper and his lever. The hero piece. A frog in an oilskin cap in the booth, one hand on a tall lever with a brass knob. One second before each fill he hauls it over: that is the warning, as every danger on the mountain warns before it acts. He sits in the house frogs' style, matte, and never leaves his booth. (1.4 to 1.9 m behind you)
  • 6The gate wheel. A big iron hand wheel on the quay's wall over the booth, its hub a pug's face. It spins as the flap gate drops and spins back as it shuts, worked by shafts in the wall: the machine you can see (rule 6). (1.5 to 1.9 m behind you)
  • 7The gauge board. A tall painted board on the back wall, from the floor to the dry shaft's top, marked every metre with a pug at the top, and a float on a guide that rides the water. Watching it tells you where the water is in its cycle. (1.5 to 1.7 m behind you)
  • 8Lanterns. Three works lanterns, teal and steady: one in the booth, one on the chamber's west wall over L1, one on the east wall over L2. (1.9 to 2.1 m behind you)

4. The climb as things

every ledge, hold, rope and mover a real object, its drawing the shape of its solid

  • 9The wicket and its sill. A small iron door in the foot of the lower gate, 2.5 m tall. It is open while the water is at the bottom, and shuts with a clang when the keeper pulls his lever. Its stone sill runs into the chamber as the lowest ledge. (in the play space)
  • 10The lower gate. The chamber's west end: a riveted iron gate from the floor to the roof, black with water and slick. At the top of a fill it holds the whole chamber back from the quay; the drain's sluices are at its foot, under the quay, and let the water out into the flume's head. (in the play space)
  • 11The two ledges. Stone corbels with pale copings on iron brackets: L1 on the west wall (x -246.6 to -245.0, top 82.4), L2 on the east wall (x -229.6 to -228.0, top 81.0). Each is too high to reach from the water at the bottom: you take it as the water brings you level. (in the play space)
  • 12The rungs. Iron staples: two on the pier's foot (y 84.6 and 85.5) and two in the dry shaft's east wall (86.8 and 87.6). Holds to wait on near the top. The top one is 2.4 m under the lip ledge: out of reach, so only the highest water gets you out. (in the play space)
  • 13The top of the dry shaft. The old dry shaft, as today: the mine's last timber set against the works' first brick, now the top of the lock. From the water at its highest you reach the lip ledge's edge, 1.5 m up, and climb out. El Borde starts there. (in the play space)

Foreground (sparse)

framing only, where nobody climbs behind it

  • 14The gate's front post. One thin timber post in front of the lower gate's edge, over solid gate, so it frames the way in and covers nothing you need (the doorway exception to the foreground rule). (1.3 to 1.6 m in front)

Small effects, no models

  • The white column of the river through the slot while it fills, foam where it hits, spray.
  • Bubbles and a swirl as the sluices drain it.
  • The clang of the wicket and the flap, the wheel's rattle.
  • Drips from the roof all the time (every rest has its unease).
