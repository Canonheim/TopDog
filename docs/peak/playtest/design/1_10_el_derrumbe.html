Diorama 1.10: El Derrumbe

The mine, the cave-in shaft with the falling rocks. A test of what one area's design looks like. Design only: nothing here has been built, and nothing is modelled until the whole mountain has been playtested.

The place, in one paragraph

This was the miners' way between two levels of the mine: a timber-lined shaft, 3.6 m wide and 15 m tall, with a hoist in the front half to haul ore up and a ladder way in the back half for the men. Then the roof came in. The falling rock went down the middle of the shaft and snapped every cross-beam on its way. What is left sticking out of the two walls are the broken ends of those beams, and they are your holds. The ladders behind are still mostly there, out of reach. The roof is still coming down.

What must not change

The play is right, so the design is built round it and moves none of it.

  • The eight timber stubs, each exactly where it is: two on the east wall, three on the west, two on the east, one on the west.
  • The shaft's width, its slick walls, the rubble slope and the fallen beam at its foot.
  • The two lines the rocks fall down, 20 cm apart in the middle of the shaft, and their rhythm (note 11).
  • Tonight's notes are folded in: the rocks leave by the snapped board (note 9, done), their smaller hit (note 10, done), and the dark chimney they come out of (note 12, sent to the thread).

The three layers

Background

  1. The chimney. The hole in the roof the rocks fall out of, black inside, with snapped roof timbers and torn planking hanging round its mouth. (Note 12.)
  2. The hoist's wheel, knocked crooked at the head of the shaft. It says what this shaft was for. No cable hangs from it: a hanging line reads as something to hold (notes 4 and 18). The cable is down at the bottom, see 6.
  3. The shaft's back wall: plank lining held by a timber frame every 1.3 m, some planks gone with dark rock showing. Two pale scars run down its middle where the rocks have been scraping it. Those scars are the warning: they mark the two lines the rocks fall down, before you have seen a rock.
  4. A lantern left on a landing. Today a lantern hangs in the very middle of the shaft, in the path of the rocks. It moves here, behind the play space, where it lights the back wall so a falling rock shows up dark against it.
  5. The ladder way. In the back half of the shaft: a half landing behind every stub, and short ladders between them, one snapped. You can see the easy way up that the miners had, and you can't get to it.
  6. The foot of the fall. The rubble heap carries on back into the scene behind the slope you walk up. Half buried in it: the hoist's crushed bucket (the kit's ore bucket) and its cable in a heap.
  7. The lower level's back wall: timber frames along the gallery, and a side tunnel boarded shut with two planks nailed across.
  8. A warning sign beside the rubble: the mine's skull-pug diamond, for falling rock. The kit has it.
  9. The upper level: timber frames, and the hand winch that worked the hoist, standing at the shaft's edge with its handle in the air.

Play space

  1. The rock between the two levels is the biggest thing on the screen and today it is a blank brown face. It gets a slanting seam of gold ore and a few old drill holes, drawn on its face.
  2. Sawn ends. Each stub's beam runs on into the rock. Where it does, its square sawn end shows on the rock's face, right beside the stub. So you can read where the holds are from the row of little squares up each wall, and the stubs plainly belong to the walls.
  3. The stubs themselves get splintered ends, and the two walls get an empty socket wherever a beam is gone altogether. The three long lunges across the shaft are at exactly those places, so the gaps in the pattern show you where the hard moves are.

Foreground (sparse)

  1. The shaft's two front posts, thin, each broken in two or three places. They stand in front of the rock at the edges of the shaft, never in front of the open shaft, so they frame the climb and cover nothing you need to see. This is your mine arch: a post behind, a post in front, and you between.
  • A low lip of dark rubble along the very bottom of the frame, below the gallery's floor.
  • A few motes of dust drifting close to the camera.
  • Nothing else. No slabs, and nothing wider than a post.

Three rules this design uses

I'd use these in every diorama. Say if any is wrong.

  1. Background wood must not look like wood you can hold. Play-space timber keeps its bright orange. The same timber in the background is pushed well toward the mine's dark grey-blue, with a thinner outline. Notes 4, 18 and 37 were all scenery that read as part of the play.
  2. Nothing in the background hangs as a thin line near where you climb. Cables, ropes and chains lie on the ground, coiled, or are simply not there.
  3. Foreground is posts and lips only, and only over solid rock or below the lowest floor (note 40). A thin post you pass behind at a doorway is the one exception.

Three things for Parker, since this is the test:

  1. Is this the right amount for one place: the paragraph, the two sketches, the three layers, the table of pieces? More, or less?
  2. Are the three rules right, especially muting the background's wood so it can't be mistaken for a hold?
  3. The front posts stand over rock at the shaft's edges. Do you want anything in front of the open shaft itself, or is "cover nothing" the rule?
