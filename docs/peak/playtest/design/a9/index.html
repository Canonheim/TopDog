Area and background design

Biome 9: El Glaciar

The glacier, 784 to 888 m. Ice that lets go, snow that falls in, and the long slide. A tongue of old ice on the east face, climbed first through the cracks inside it and then up its outer skin.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

Above the cableway's high station the east face carries a glacier: a tongue of old ice a hundred metres tall, lying on the rock and creeping downhill too slowly to see. Its outer skin is steep, smooth and dark, and it ends in a lip over the cableway's void. Whatever comes off that skin slides the whole length of it and is thrown off the lip. So the way up starts inside, through the cracks.

Nobody builds on ice that moves. The cableway stops at the station, and from there up nothing is built at all. What is here was carried in by the luchadores who came to climb: rope, axes, candle lanterns, canes with paper flags to mark the way. Many of them never came out. The ice closed over them and has carried them downhill ever since, so the walls of the cracks have climbers in them, frozen as they were, most of them reaching up.

The ice is busy in its slow way. It grows icicles and drops them, raises a tower and lets it fall, sends slabs of snow down its drain, and makes every one of them again. Nothing it makes can be trusted for long. The only things here that never let go of you are the stones it carries, the ropes that were left, and the ones who tried. They are the way up: through the lower crack, the ice cave and the level above it, out of a window in the skin, and up the outside in the wind to the gap where the ice has pulled away from the ridge's rock.

How it is built

  • Ice is cut, never moulded. The glacier's body is blue-black and glossy, in flat planes 2 to 4 m across that meet at hard angles, with thin white crack lines. It is flush: nothing sticks out of it that is not a hold.
  • Blue ice is old hard ice that runs through the body in seams. It is pale blue, frosted and matte, with white frost along its top edge. In a back wall a seam is a band half a metre wide. Where it reaches the play space it stands 0.3 m proud as knuckles, nubs and plates, and those are the holds. A seam never widens into anything shaped like a ramp or a ledge (note 84).
  • The ice is layered. Bands run through every back wall, pale and dark by turns, tilted about ten degrees down to the east, the way the ice flows. Stones ride in the bands: grey-brown and dry, from the size of a fist to 1.2 m. They are the only rock until the ridge.
  • Snow lies only on top of things. Firm snow is white and matte, 0.4 to 0.7 m thick, with a square front edge and the ice plainly under it. A snow floor is never blank for more than 4 m: it has blue hollows, wind ridges, and a line of old boot prints going the way the route goes.
  • A room inside the ice is a crack seen from the side with its near wall taken away. Its back wall is the ice itself, 2.4 to 4 m behind the climb, and it never stops inside the frame. Light shows the way through: daylight comes into a room only at its way out, so the way out is the palest thing in the room, and it can be seen from where you came in. A roof you cannot pass is dark and unbroken from wall to wall. A shelf you must go round is broken off short, and the gap past its broken end is open and lit from above.
  • The lower crack (challenges 9.1 to 9.3) is redone to that rule (note 86), and is not made easier. It is one zigzag in one tall slot: the blue seam climbs west to a corner ledge, the row of big icicles comes back east under a shelf that is broken off short, and the snow bridges carry on east from the shelf's top to the lit door of the ice cave. The door shows from the floor where you come in.
  • Made things are only what a climber could carry: hemp rope, ice axes with wooden shafts, candle lanterns, wool, leather, canes with paper flags, one painted sign. No timber frames, no brick, no steel, no carved stone, nothing bolted to the ice.
  • Nothing made of ice, snow or cloud ever stands in front of where a climber can be (notes 87 and 92).

Colour and light

Blue shadow inside, white glare outside. Inside, the only light is daylight coming through the ice: the thinner the ice, the paler the wall. Each room has one pale place, its way out, and its closed corners are deep blue. This is shown by colour, never by glow. The few lanterns are the only warm light: small and amber, one at each door on the side you go through, and one in the frog's snow hole. Outside it is the cableway's gold light gone whiter and harder: white snow, blue-black ice, long blue shadows. From 860 m up the cloud is close overhead and the light goes flat and grey, ready for the storm.

The tower of El Serac falls all the way into its pit and shatters there; the next one grows up from the stump, bare and dark, gets its snow last, and only then can be climbed (note 88). It blowsLong wavy white lines across the air, each with a dark inked edge so it shows against snow, running the way the wind runs (note 89). Flags say it too: the paper flags on the route's canes, the town's torn paper at the window, a climber's scarf, the frayed end of the old rope, and snow smoking off every edge. Before the wind changes sides the flags drop slack and then lift the new way, and a front of lines crosses from the side it is coming from.
The ones who triedOne you can hold stands whole in the play space, out of the ice, with the ice at his back and nothing in front of him (note 87). His body and mask are frost grey. His boots, belt and mittens are warm: honey leather, hemp rope, bone-white wool, each a lump 0.35 m across, exactly the size of its grip. One mitten is always raised, and it points at the next hold. The first of each group has a lit lantern beside him, lighting him and not the wall. One in the scenery is a flat blue shape deep inside the ice, any way up, with no warm colour on him.
The way onThe palest place in the room, seen from where you came in: a door, a window or a gap with daylight in it and frost round its edge. A lantern hangs on the side you go through, and the boot prints in the snow lead to the first hold. Where the route turns back on itself there is one pug arrow sign on a post: on the corner ledge of Carámbanos, pointing east (note 85). Outside, paper flags on canes mark the way.
It is sceneryInside: the crack's far wall, mid blue, dull and flat, in bands, 2.4 m or more back. It is never pale blue, never has snow on it, and never has a shape you could stand on. Outside: the glacier's far side as one flat painted card, greyer and hazed, and then the valley, the ranges and the cloud.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: From the cableway's high station at 786 m. Its deck is the last made floor on the mountain: red steel, cream paint and planks under a skin of pale blue ice that already lets go of you. The glacier's lip hangs over the deck's west end with a fringe of icicles, and you walk in under it: an alcove, then a passage of blue ice 21 m long and 4.8 m high with a firm snow floor, which opens into the lower crack. Gold light turns to blue shadow. The last pennant is frozen stiff, and the first cane with a paper flag stands in the alcove.

Leaving: By one fling, from the raised mitten of the climber frozen on the glacier's very top: 7 m west and 3.4 m up, across the gap where the ice has pulled away from the ridge. Under you the gap drains into the chute and the whole slide. You land on a snow ledge at 886 m, on dark wet slate, under a slick prow of rock that roofs it. The ledge's first metre is glazed, so you land past that. The ledge is the storm ridge's: note 94 makes it a little cabin with a frog, and that is for the storm ridge's sheet. On this side the ice ends, the light is already grey, the ridge's first prayer pole stands on the ledge with its paper streaming, and nothing stands in front of the last climber's mitten (note 92).

Running through it

  • The ones who tried: here they matter most. Seven are the way up, and more are deep in the walls. One is frozen a hand's width under the lip he almost climbed out over. The last leans out from the very top, over the gap, and his mitten is the launch.
  • The slide: the long dark skin under every outside move, all the way down to the lip over the cableway.
  • The pug: nothing is built here, so it shows only on what was carried: the masks of the ones who tried, and the one arrow sign.
  • The town follows you up: torn paper over the window in the skin, and paper flags on the canes, streaming. From the window on, the town is tiny in the valley below.
  • The frog: in a snow hole at the east end of the ice cave's floor, wrapped in a blanket beside a lantern and a kettle. You see its warm light far below as you fly from the slide to the rope, and a miss there drops you near its door.

  • Note 84: pale blue ice lets go after 2 seconds. The tell is the button mark on the holding limb, which shivers harder until it loses its grip. The pale ramp behind Hielo Azul, which could not be stood on, becomes a thin seam in the back wall.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
