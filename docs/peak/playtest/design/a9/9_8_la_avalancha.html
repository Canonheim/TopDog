El Glaciar · Area and background design

Challenge 9.8: La Avalancha

Design only. Nothing here is built, and nothing is modelled until the whole mountain has been playtested.

The place, in one paragraph

Over the niche is the mouth of the chute: the drain of the gap between the glacier and the ridge. It is a trough of ice 50 degrees steep, roofed for its first 12 m by the foot of the glacier's top tower. Snow slabs let go under the ridge every five and a half seconds. They come out of a hole in the ice wall at the very top, slide down the open part of the chute, and at the foot of an old rope a flake of ice throws each one sideways into a hollow in the back wall, where it bursts. None comes further. What comes down the roofed part, where you climb, is the snow dust of each burst. The climb is the chute's floor: plates of blue ice, and two long stones frozen into the floor with ice glazed over their backs. Under the downhill end of each stone the rock is bare, and those are the two places to rest. Then the rope: 5.4 m of frozen hemp that somebody left, up to a niche in the tower's stem.

What must not change

  • The tusk under the chute's mouth, the three nubs round the nose, and the eight plates of blue ice up the floor, each where it is.
  • The two long stones, with the glaze over their backs and the bare rock under their downhill ends.
  • The old rope, its stone horn, and the niche in the stem at 867.8 m.
  • The slabs' timing: one every 5.5 seconds, with its warning. Notes 91 and 93 change where a slab starts and where it ends, and nothing else.
  • The fall: down the chute, out of its mouth, the slide.

The three layers

Background

  • 1The cloud and the valley. What is given. From 860 m up the cloud is close overhead and the light is flat and grey. Between the tower's tip and the ridge it is all cloud. (7.8 to 8.0 m behind you)
  • 2The glacier's far side. The same painted card as behind Ventisca, 45 m back, its edge of leaning towers showing east of the chute's mouth and the tower's stem. (7.9 to 8.0 m behind you)
  • 3The ridge's rock, across the gap. Behind the open part of the chute: the mountain itself, 2.4 m back. Dark wet slate in long leaning slabs, dull blue-grey, with rain marks. It is the first rock since the cableway, and it is the storm ridge's own piece. Today a flat grey card stands here. (2.4 to 4.0 m behind you)
  • 4The ice behind the roofed chute and the niches. Behind the roofed part of the chute and behind each niche: the glacier's banded ice, dark, because the tower's foot is over it. Against it the pale plates of the climb show plainly. A scatter of smaller stones stands in it behind the two long ones: they are the biggest of one layer of stones. (2.4 to 3.0 m behind you)
  • 5The slab hole. A dark mouth 1.4 m across in the ice wall at the top of the chute, under the ridge's ledge, with a lip of packed snow (note 93). It is the end of the crack that runs along the foot of the ridge, where the ridge's snow piles up. You see each slab in it before it comes. (1.2 to 3.4 m behind you)
  • 6The hollow the slabs are thrown into. A hollow 4 m wide and 3 m deep in the back wall, beside the foot of the rope, where a slab of the slate has fallen out (note 91). Each snow slab is thrown up into it and bursts there. A heap of broken snow lies in its floor, and a white fan of burst marks covers its back. (2.4 to 5.4 m behind you)
  • 7A cane in each niche. At the back of the niche under the chute's mouth and of the niche in the stem: a cane with a paper flag. In the niches the flags hang slack, and that is how you know a niche is out of the wind. (1.5 to 1.8 m behind you)
  • 8The rest of the rope. At the back of the stem's niche: a coil of the same hemp frozen flat, and an ice axe. Whoever left the rope sat here. (1.3 to 1.8 m behind you)

Play space

  • 9The kicker at the rope's foot. New, from note 91: a flake of the glacier's dark ice wedged across the chute 2.4 m uphill of the rope, its face 1.8 m long and turned toward the back wall. Every slab rides up it and is thrown into the hollow. It is glossy and slick, the same piece as the slide's kicker, and it is above the last hold of the climb.
  • 10A slab's whole road. A slab shows in the hole's mouth, creeps out during its warning, tips onto the chute, slides 20 m down the open part, hits the kicker and is thrown up and back. All of it is in view from the climb. The dashed line is that road.
  • 11A slab in the mouth. A block of white snow 1 m across, exactly the size that is solid, with a square broken edge.
  • 12The two rests. Ringed: the bare rock under the downhill end of each long stone. It is grey-brown and dry, under a little roof of its own stone. With no slab coming down this far, these are plain rests: the two places on the climb where a hand can stay. The stones' backs keep their glaze, and the plates on them are still blue ice.
  • 13The tusk under the mouth. The first hold out of the niche: a blunt hanging point of blue ice, the same piece as at the window.
  • 14The old rope. Hemp, frozen stiff, tied to a horn of grey stone at the stem's corner. Its frayed end hangs 1.7 m above the chute's floor and streams in whatever wind there is: another flag.

Small effects, no models

  • A slab coming: a creak in the hole and a tick, the slab creeping out, a hiss down the chute, a thump on the kicker, a burst in the hollow.
  • After each burst, snow dust pours down the roofed chute past you and out of its mouth. It pushes nothing. It is the avalanche you climb under.
  • Fine snow blowing down the whole gap all the time.
  • The rope's frayed end and the niches' flags.

Tonight's notes folded in

  • Note 93: the slabs come out of a hole in the wall at the top of the chute, and each is seen in the hole before it comes.
  • Note 91: a kicker at the rope's foot throws each slab into the background, into a hollow where it bursts. No slab reaches the climb below.
  • Course change, as those two notes ask: one new slick flake on the chute's floor, from x -290.8 to x -289.05, 2.4 m uphill of where the rope hangs and above the last hold. The slabs start at the wall, 1.3 m west of where they start today.
  • Note 91: the two long stones shelter you from nothing now, so they are plain rests. They do not move and are not made bigger.
  • The name: the avalanche is still here to see and hear. It comes down the open chute in view, bursts beside the rope, and its dust comes down over the climb.
  • Note 84: the button mark shivers on a limb that holds blue ice. It does not on the stones or the rope.
