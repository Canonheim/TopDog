Area and background design

Biome 7: Los Riscos del Viento

The wind cliffs, 570 to 690 m. The mountain's east face, and the first open air since the station. Chalk, dead wood, vultures, and wind you can see.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

At 580 m you come out of the inside of the mountain. This is its east face: a cliff of chalk that the weather has split into a tower, an arch, fins, needles and one lone horn. There are 120 m of it to climb, and under most of it there is nothing but La Garganta, the throat. Only the vultures live here by choice.

People came this way once, on the outside. When the town built its cableway, the timber, the cable and the paint went up this face on burros, along a pack trail of planks, rope and iron pegs: a rope bridge from the arch to the tower, a rock fence across the gully, a catwalk under the station, a drivers' shelter cut into the rock, and a flag at every corner where the wind could take a loaded animal.

Then the wind and the rockfalls took the trail apart. The bridge is out and broken, most of the pegs are gone, and below the Balcón the scree has taken all of it. The burros still stand on the ledges where the trail left them. The flags still fly, and they are the one thing on the face that tells you what the wind is about to do.

How it is built

  • The rock is chalk in level beds, split by upright joints, so it breaks into flat ledges, towers and thin fins. Where it lies flat or is freshly broken it is dry and white: ledge tops, the lips of cracks, knobs, bands. Everywhere else its face is dark brown, polished to a gloss by rain and wind.
  • The rock is painted, not built of blocks, as the worked example of the tower top shows. Each face is a few large slanted planes of dark brown. The chalk's beds are long wavy lines and bands of flat colour on it, in three or four browns, running the length of the face. There are no rain streaks, and nothing painted narrows to a point. A white hold is one of the rock's own beds, standing proud where it is held: a plain block or band with square ends.
  • Only a few big breaks are real shape: one bed that overhangs with its shadow painted under it, one opened joint, one ledge with grass and rubble on it, one leaning slab, a broken tip. The outline leans, tapers and is notched where a piece has gone, with no long ruled edge. No square panel is repeated, and no bed is built as its own block. The holds and the tops you stand on stay exactly where they are.
  • Dead wood is bleached silver white, smooth, with the bark gone except in strips. A lightning scar is black and glassy. Nothing here is green: dry grass in tufts, and one dry shrub.
  • The pack trail was made from what a burro can carry: split planks in honey timber, hemp rope, poles lashed with rope, and rust-orange iron pegs and brackets hammered into the rock. Rope is knotted to posts. Nothing is square, and everything has been mended at least once.
  • The station's scaffold is the first sawn and painted timber since the town: square posts in yellow ochre and braces in the cableway's red, bolted through iron plates with the pug's face on them. The bull wheel is red with cream spokes, and it is drawn 7.6 m across.
  • A flag is a pole in red and white bands with a long streamer in the town's bunting colours. Flags stand at the back of ledges, behind the climb.
  • Never here: brick, carved stone, black forge iron, crystal, a lit lantern outside the tea cave, or any cloud of our own.

Colour and light

Hard blue daylight, the first since the station. The sun is high, so the face is bright, shadows are short and hard, and the undersides of the arch, the cornice and every roof are in cool blue shade. The sky is the biggest thing on screen. The only warm light is in the tea cave: amber from a small lantern and a brazier, shown as colour on its walls. By the low station the daylight has begun to turn toward the cableway's gold.

The three layers in this biome

LayerWhat goes in it here
BackgroundNear, 1.5 to 3 m back: the body of the rock you are on. Every tower, arch, fin, needle and horn you climb is the near edge of grey-tan cliff that carries on behind it, with beds, joints, small ledges, nests with white streaks under them, bone piles, grass tufts, short dead trees and the trail's leavings. Middle, 12 m and more back, on the west half of the frame only: the mountain's shoulder, one flat cut-out card of the same cliff, paler and bluer, so the face goes on behind you and never stops inside the frame. East of the route: open sky, straight through to the far view that is given (the painted valley with the tiny town, the two painted ranges, the cloud banks above). Nothing of ours stands in front of it.
Play spaceChalk ledges, cracks and knobs; the dead tree and the snag; the vultures; the hanging halves of the bridge; the rock fence's rails; the iron pegs; the burros; the perch plank; the rock chimney's bands; the planks, the wheel's rim and the long brace of the scaffold.
ForegroundVery little, and never in front of open air, because the air is where you read a fall. A chalk lip with a grass tuft at a bottom corner, in front of solid rock. One trail post with a rag of bunting at the edge of the Balcón's first screen. At the station, one scaffold post at the frame's west edge, in front of the deck. A few wind lines pass in front of you.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: Out of the chimney's crack (challenge 6.9), through a door in the face and down 2 m onto El Balcón, a chalk shelf 22 m long. The basalt and the last red light are behind you. In front are blue air, the valley with the town tiny below, a pug arrow pointing east along the shelf, and 9 m on, the first flag, already moving.

Leaving: By challenge 7.10, La Estación Baja, to the low cableway station at 688 m. From the top of the tower's west fin you cross the pylon platform, ride the bull wheel's rim over the top, and climb the scaffold's long brace to the plank deck. Our end of the join is plain: a painted timber scaffold with pug plates at its joints, the red bull wheel 7.6 m across with the haul cable running over it, and a deck of honey planks 14 m long with a line of pennants and a cold lantern. The chalk ends at the deck and the light has started to turn gold. The cableway is the next biome.

Running through it

  • The town below: seen for the first time since the station, tiny, in the painted valley. On the Balcón the camera looks down so that it sits low in the frame.
  • The town follows you up: its bunting is the wind's flag here. Every streamer is in the town's colours, and a whole line of pennants hangs along the station's deck.
  • The pug: painted on the trail's arrow sign, on the iron plates at the scaffold's joints, and on the bull wheel's hub.
  • The ones who tried: one frozen climber sitting at the back of the Balcón, facing the town.
  • The burros: the trail's own, still standing where it left them.
  • The mercy: one dry shrub on the Balcón's east lip, over the throat.
  • The frog: at tea in the drivers' cave, with a second cup set out for you. A second frog sits in the vulture's nest.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
  13. Walls and rock are painted, not built of blocks (Parker, 11-13: "I like the painted look"). A wall, a cliff or any mass you climb is a few big planes with a designed, uneven outline. Its layers and courses are flat painted colour on those planes, as long wavy lines and bands, with no ink inside a face and nothing that tapers to a point (no streaks or wedges: "it does not need these random triangles ... just the painted lines look lovely"). Only a few big breaks are real shape: an overhang, a deep recess, a ledge, a leaning slab. No repeated square panel, no long ruled edge. The holds and the tops you stand on stay exactly where the course has them. The mass's own body carries on behind it, duller, as one big form. Worked example: the split tower top of the wind cliffs (design/walls_example_before.jpg and walls_example_after.png).
