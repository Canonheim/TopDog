Area and background design

Biome 8: El Teleférico

The abandoned cableway, 684 to 790 m. Moving to moving, in the open air off the east face, with nothing under you. From here a miss is the whole mountain.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

Above the cliffs the east face is loose scree that nothing can be fixed to. So the town's cableway leaves the mountain. From the low station at the cliff top it swings out east over La Garganta to El Colmillo, the rock fang, and comes back west and up to the high station at the foot of the glacier. It was the town's proudest work: red and cream paint, the pug on every door, a ticket booth, pennants on every cable.

It runs on wind. A cup wheel on each tower catches the same wind that blows across the cliffs and turns the wheels through a belt, so no fuel ever had to come up the pack trail. Then one winter the ice came down over the high station and a cable snapped, the trail below fell apart, and the town stopped coming. Nobody stopped the cableway, because nothing stops it but a calm day.

What is left is a machine going round by itself: cars that pass an empty dock every six seconds, chairs swinging on a dead line, counterweights rising and falling, one car hanging dead by one arm. Between the low station's deck and the high station's there are 98 m of height and no floor but the heads of the towers.

How it is built

  • Steel, riveted: angle iron in lattice, with a plate at every joint and the pug stamped on the plate. A tower is two legs 3 m apart with zigzag braces in bays 4 m tall. All of it is painted the cableway's red, sun-faded, peeling in flakes to rust orange.
  • Everything hangs from behind. Cables run 2 m behind the climb, 0.15 m thick, black with grease. A hanger comes down from its cable and cranks forward to the back of what it carries. You hold what is carried, never what carries it: no cable, hanger or tower leg stands in the play space. Pylon 2's two greased legs are the one exception.
  • What is drawn is what can be touched (notes 76, 77). Every piece you can hold is modelled at the exact size the course gives it, with every solid part on it: a car's step and rack, a wheel's whole rim. A part that is not solid is not modelled in the play space.
  • Cream marks the hold. The edge you take on a moving thing has a cream band: a car's roof edge, floor edge, step and rack, a chair's seat front, the zip handle, a wheel's spokes. Cream is never painted on scenery.
  • Floors are few and small: honey planks on steel for the station decks and tower heads, iron grating for landings, one rail post on each. A station house is board walls, red below cream, under a striped tin roof, and it stands back from its deck.
  • El Colmillo is the cliffs' own rock: dark and rain-polished, with white chalk only in knobs. Its top carries one iron plate and one post.
  • Never here: timber framing (the scaffold stops at the low station), rope, brick, carved stone, anything green, a lit lantern outside the frog's car, icicles (they are the glacier's to teach), or a cable you can hold.

Colour and light

Late-afternoon gold. The sun is low over the valley, so everything has a warm lit side and a long blue-violet shadow side, and the sky runs from apricot at the bottom to pale gold. Gold dust drifts in the air between the towers. The station lanterns are cold: the only lamp alight on the whole cableway is in the frog's car, shown as amber colour on its walls. At the high station you climb into the mountain's own shadow: the gold stays on the towers behind you, the deck is blue, and the first snowflakes fall.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: From the cliffs by challenge 7.10: up the scaffold's long brace onto the low station's deck at 688 m, 14 m of honey planks that the cliffs' file builds. Our end of the join: the boarding gangway on the deck's east end, 0.7 m higher, with one rail post; the station house 2.5 m back with no roof over the deck; and the cliffs' haul cable coming up from the bull wheel to the station's wind engine. The last chalk is the plug at the deck's west end, the light turns gold, and a car comes down to the gangway every six seconds, in sight the whole way round its loop.

Leaving: Off the fourth express car onto the high station's deck at 786 m: 14 m of planks against the face, under a tongue of ice with three dry patches. It is the first ice on the climb. Our end of the join: the same station house as below, 2.5 m back and half buried in snow, its wind engine frozen still, its lantern cold, the deck in blue shadow and the first snowflakes falling. Steel, paint and planks end where the deck's west end meets rock. The glacier, the next biome, begins on and above this deck.

Running through it

  • Nothing under you: the first place where the frame holds no ground at all, only the valley far below.
  • The town below: tiny, in the painted valley, straight under the cars.
  • The town follows you up: pennant lines on every cable, more bunting than anywhere since the station. They all point east, so they are also how you see the wind.
  • The pug: a peeling crest on every gondola's door, on the station signs, on the hub of every wheel, on every joint plate and counterweight.
  • The ones who tried: one frozen climber sitting at the back of pylon 3's head, looking at the dead gondola. A frozen glove on the high station's deck.
  • The wind that pushed you on the cliffs works here: it is the cableway's engine.
  • The frog: the last passenger, in a parked gondola behind pylon 1's perch, under the only lit lantern on the cableway.

Tonight's notes that change this biome

  • Note 76: the bull wheel's solid rim was bigger than the wheel drawn. The wheel you ride is the cliffs'. Here the lesson is a rule for every moving piece, and each one's kit line says which parts are held.
  • Note 77: the low station's roof looked holdable and was not, so the station house stands 2.5 m back with no roof over the deck. Every gondola's step and rack were solid and not drawn, so they are drawn.
  • Note 78: the sheave carried you into a pinch, and a crunch must knock you out. With the cable and the two bars gone, nothing stands near the sheaves to be crushed against. Any pinch an area keeps wears the warning stripes.
  • Note 79: El Cable comes out. It is one long jump from the gondola's rack to pylon 2's landing. The cable has snapped: a short end hangs from each tower, out of reach.
  • Note 80: the chairs' hanger poles become background and only the seats are held. Our call from it: every hanger on the cableway is background, the gondolas' too. Pylon 1's perch is reached today by climbing a car's hanger, so it gets one iron rung under its east end.
  • Note 81: El Pilón loses its brace and its rung: landing, wheel, wheel, head.
  • Note 82: the zip line runs all the way to the maintenance deck and stops against a sprung buffer.
  • Note 83: held between two moving things, you keep the one more of your limbs are on, or the higher one. A rule of play, with no look of its own.
  • Note 89: wind must be far more obvious. Parker: 'i like the flag and wavy wind idea'. Pennants, streamers and wind socks show this biome's steady wind; the wavy lines are kept for a wind that pushes.
  • Note 60: a frog in every biome. Here it is the last passenger, in a parked gondola off the route.
  • Parker, 11-13, on the area's old stand-in far cards: 'This will be addressed once we build that area as it is that area's background'. This sheet's answer: they stay out. Sky, the mountain's shoulder on the west, and the dead far line are all that stand in front of the far view.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
