El Templo del Primer Perro · Area and background design

Challenge 5.3: Los Pistones

Design only. Nothing here is built, and nothing is modelled until the whole mountain has been playtested.

The place, in one paragraph

Past the mask's brow the walk goes under a great stone pier and ends at a shaft, 5.5 m wide and 16 m tall, that goes up into the temple's works. The shaft is a machine. A plunger in its floor lifts you to where the walls begin, and then nine stone bolts, each 3.4 m long, slide out of the two walls in turn, bottom to top, like the bolts of a great lock being thrown one after another. The walls themselves are polished black. The only way up is on the bolts, and each bolt, as it comes out, pushes off whoever is still standing in front of it. West of the plunger the floor is missing: that is the throat that drops to the chute.

What must not change

  • The nine pistons: which wall each is in, their heights 1.2 m apart, how far they come out, and their order and timing.
  • The plunger's 3.6 m lift, and the open throat west of it.
  • The walls can't be held, and a piston that is in sits 0.33 m inside the wall's face (note 48).
  • The shaft is narrow and you read both walls at once, so nothing stands in front of it.

The three layers

Background

  • 1The shaft's back wall. Fitted blocks, a shade browner than the bolts, 2.2 m behind you. It carries on down behind the throat into the dark, east under the pier behind the walk, and up into the gear room, so there is no black anywhere in the frame (note 51). (2.2 to 2.7 m behind you)
  • 2Nine tracks. Each piston is the near end of a long bolt, and its back half runs in a track cut across the back wall and lined with bronze. Five tracks reach out from the west wall and four from the east. Each ends in a bronze stop exactly where that piston's tip stops. You can read the whole ladder, and how far every step will come, before anything moves. (1.2 to 2.2 m behind you)
  • 3The plunger's guide. A slot up the back wall behind the plunger, 3.6 m taller than the plunger itself. The empty part above it is the height it will lift you. (1.2 to 2.2 m behind you)
  • 4The carved band. Along the top of the wall under the pier, where you read it as you walk to the plunger: small figures stepping from bolt to bolt, left wall then right wall, and one being pushed off. (2.2 to 2.7 m behind you)
  • 5An offering niche. Beside the plunger, at head height: a small square niche holding a bone and a jade bead. The temple's people left one beside every machine. (2.2 to 2.7 m behind you)
  • 6Torches. One over the throat, one under the pier, one at the head of the shaft. None sits between the tracks, so nothing bright is behind a piston. (2.0 to 2.3 m behind you)

Play space

  • 7Nine sockets. On the face of each wall, the mouth each piston lives in: a dark square in a carved frame. The game draws these today (note 48). A socket with nothing in it is plainly not a hold.
  • 8Polished black walls. Both walls are polished black from the plunger's top to the head of the shaft, with pale runs of damp down them. Nothing on them is pale except the pistons.
  • 9Bronze faces and jade bands. Each piston's end is shod in dark bronze, the temple's mark for what hurts: that is the face that shoves you. Its top is pale stone with a jade band. The plunger wears the same band round its top.

Foreground (sparse)

Nothing.

Small effects, no models

  • A grind and a puff of dust at a socket half a second before its piston starts back in (note 48).
  • A line of dust falls from a track's stop when the piston hits it.
  • Grit trickles down the throat now and then, so you see it is a hole.

Tonight's notes folded in

  • Note 48 (done): the pistons sit a third of a metre inside the wall when in, the walls can't be held, a hand on a piston going in is let go at the wall's face, and the socket warns before it moves. The sockets are item 7 here.
  • Note 48 asked that the walls read as walls you can't hold. Here they are the only polished black in the shaft, and the back wall is plainly different stone.
