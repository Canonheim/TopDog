Area and background design

Biome 5: El Templo del Primer Perro

The buried temple of the First Top Dog, 364 to 484 m. Carved stone and stone machinery, by torchlight.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

Long before the town, people built a temple inside the mountain to the First Top Dog: the first luchador to climb the Peak, a pug in a mask. They cut it out of the rock west of the well, hall above hall, and they filled it with tests. To reach the top of the temple you had to get past its machines.

The machines still run. Nobody knows what turns them. Steps slide into the wall, pistons push out, gears mesh, censers swing, a cross sweeps the altar's shaft. The torches are lit. The banners are fresh.

It is the grandest place on the mountain and the only one that was made to be climbed. Every challenge here was put here on purpose by somebody.

How it is built

  • Big blocks of pale stone, fitted without mortar, joints tight. Walls step back as they rise, in a sloped base with a flat band above it. Doorways and passages are stepped arches: two sides stepping in to a flat lintel.
  • A carved band runs along every wall at the same heights: pug faces, belts and bones. It never shows how a challenge is done (Parker, 11-13). Where you can hold the wall, the carving is deep and pale. Where you can't, the stone is polished black with soot and damp.
  • Jade green is the temple's accent: inlaid eyes, a stripe on a banner, a band round anything that moves.
  • Gold caps the things that matter: the mask's teeth, the altar, the censers' lids.
  • Stone machinery. Every moving block has a slot it slides in, and a bronze-shod edge; every wheel has a stone axle in two carved cheeks; every swinging thing hangs by bronze chain from a carved pug boss.
  • Banners of cut paper in the town's colours, whole and bright, hung high. Torches in bronze wall brackets.

Colour and light

Torch gold. A torch every few metres on the walls you climb, so the play space is warm and clearly lit, and the halls behind fall away into brown shadow with far torches as points. Jade and gold catch the light. No daylight, no glow.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: Out of the air, from the second well crossing: you catch one of the two banner chains hanging down the well's west wall and climb onto the temple's doorstep. Roots and small mushrooms have got into the first few blocks. Green light gives way to torch gold.

Leaving: By the third crossing, over the well's own mouth, on the two giant censers. The east side is the forge: the same stone, black with soot, then iron grates and red light from below.

Running through it

  • The pug: everywhere, and biggest here. The First Top Dog's mask is three storeys tall.
  • The ones who tried: a chain of three frozen climbers hanging hand to ankle off a ledge. They nearly made a ladder.
  • The town follows you up: the banners are the town's cut paper, and here they are whole.
  • The frog: on the offering table of a small side shrine, where a carved pug should be.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
