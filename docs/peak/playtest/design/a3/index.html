Area and background design

Biome 3: La Gruta de Cristal

The crystal cave, 176 to 276 m. Glass and light. The first place on the climb that nobody built.

Design only. This sheet comes first: every area in the biome is drawn from it.

What this place is

Above the dam the mountain is hollow and full of crystal: points the size of houses, growing out of the walls, the roof and the floor. Water did this, over more time than the town has existed. The mine company never got up here, and nobody else ever built anything here either.

So nothing in the cave is made. What moves here moves by itself: a geode that turns because water runs through it, a great quartz that swings because the mountain breathes, collars of crystal that have grown round a column, with spikes that slide out and in. The cave is strange the way a living thing is strange, and nothing in it explains itself with a bolt or a chain.

The only signs of people are the ones who tried: one frozen climber, and a coil of old rope.

How it is built

  • Crystal grows in points. Six-sided in section, ending in a point, always leaning the way it grew. Crystals jut out of the back wall towards you (note 26), out of the floor and down from the roof. Nothing in the cave is a flat-topped block or a plain hexagon post.
  • Crystals come in clusters: one big point with smaller ones round its root, on a collar of rough dark rock.
  • Two kinds of crystal. Rough crystal is pale lilac, frosted, and looks like rock: you can hold it (note 30). Clear crystal is deep indigo glass, mirror-smooth: you can't.
  • What hangs, hangs on crystal: a thread of crystal as thick as an arm, grown down from the roof like a stalactite and still joined at both ends. What turns, turns in a socket of crystal worn round and smooth. No chain, no bronze, no carved stone.
  • No timber, no brick, no iron, nothing carved. One coil of old rope and one frozen climber are the only things here that came from outside.

Colour and light

Violet and ice-white. The light comes from inside the crystals: big far ones glow dimly, and each area has one or two bright white ones that light it. No lanterns, no torches. The background is deep indigo, and the things you hold are the palest things on screen. One place has other colours: behind El Prisma's shelves a faint fan of rainbow lies on the wall.

How you come in, and how you leave

Coming in: From the dam's crest. Crystals have grown down through the top of the brickwork and split it. Two steps up, the brick is gone and the floor is crystal. Teal light turns violet.

Leaving: By the first crossing of the well, east from a crystal horn on the west rim. On the far rim the garden has already started: roots hang into the shaft and there is moss in the cracks. You leave pale violet and land in green.

Running through it

  • Your own reflection, small, in the mirror faces behind you.
  • Nobody built this: after two biomes of timber and brick, there is not one made thing.
  • The ones who tried: one frozen climber.
  • The well: the first of the three crossings.
  • The frog: inside a split geode, sitting among the crystals.

Rules that hold on the whole mountain

  1. What you can hold is dry, pale and matte. What you can't is dark, glossy and wet. This is the mountain's first law and no design breaks it.
  2. Where a thing stands tells you more than its colour (Parker, 11-13). If it is in the play space you can generally hold it. Scenery stays out of the play space, behind or in front, and is greyer, darker and cooler than the things you climb.
  3. Everything you touch is the near end of something real that carries on behind it, like a ladder and its rungs.
  4. Nothing thin hangs in the background near the climb: it reads as a rope (notes 4 and 18). A hanging thing is a real rope, or thick, or far back.
  5. The foreground is sparse: thin posts and low lips, in front of solid rock or below the lowest floor, never over the open space you climb through (note 40).
  6. Every moving thing shows its machine: what drives it stands in the background (notes 19, 36, 57).
  7. Every danger shows where it comes from and warns before it acts (notes 12, 21, 23, 56).
  8. Every breakable thing wears the game's one breakable look: cracked already, with a corner gone (notes 16, 24, 31, 53, 64).
  9. The painted back wall never stops inside the frame, so there are no black holes (note 51). The insides of small pits can't be held (note 52).
  10. One frog is hidden in every biome (note 60), in a little room you can walk into. A room you can enter is cut away at the front: floor, back wall and roof, no front wall.
  11. Far backgrounds are flat facade pieces (Parker, 11-13): one cut-out card standing well back, painted in a few broad rows that haze toward the horizon, with a few bold silhouettes cut into its ridge. Never modelled terrain, and never an empty plane running to a ruled horizon. The station's dry hills are the worked example.
  12. Open ground is designed, never one flat colour (Parker, 11-13): one painted slab with no height that runs off every edge of the screen, laid out as a real place in bands from the camera back (tufts and stones, a worn road with ruts, a trodden yard, paving where people built, patched open country, paler far bands), with real plants and rocks standing on its darker patches. No more than about 4 m of one flat colour on screen. The station's ground is the worked example.
